# Brief C7: the optional chapters on the front page and in the links between lessons

You are drafting words for the front page of an interactive course, *Digital Design: From Bits to
a Working Computer*. Read `docs/style.md` in the repository before you write: it is the style
checklist, and it applies to every sentence. Use only the facts here. Where a sentence seems to
need a fact you were not given, write a note in square brackets instead of inventing it. Do not
write or change any file. Return each item under its number, with no commentary.

## Why

The course has 14 numbered modules, 0 to 13, which end with a whole working machine. Two optional
chapters now come after Module 13, under the heading "Beyond the machine". The front page needs a
line for them after the five stages, and the way in and the links between lessons need words that
name them.

## Voice

British English. Direct, plain, active, short. The reader is "you". No marketing tone, no
exclamation marks, no rhetorical questions, no filler, no em dashes or en dashes used as dashes.
Numbers as digits.

## Words the front page must not use

A reader meets the front page before every lesson, so no item may use any of these words, nor
their plurals or other forms (for example "instructions", "gates", "compiled"): bit, threshold,
noise margin, binary, word, unsigned, signed, two's complement, hexadecimal, gate, truth table,
Boolean expression, XOR, SystemVerilog, NAND, universal, depth, multiplexer, bus, decoder,
demultiplexer, encoder, comparator, carry, half adder, full adder, overflow, XNOR, ALU, feedback,
latch, transparent, edge, propagation delay, setup, hold, metastable, register, shift register,
counter, register transfer, state machine, state diagram, next-state logic, one-hot, synchronous,
address, RAM, register file, byte, aligned, alignment, memory-mapped, ROM, flag, boundary test,
adversarial test, instruction, datapath, program counter, fetch, branch, control unit, illegal
instruction, instruction register, micro-operation, instruction set, microarchitecture, opcode,
immediate, assembly, assembler, debugger, breakpoint, function, argument, calling convention,
stack, recursion, trap, handler, user mode, system mode, system call, interrupt, CPU, machine
code, abstraction, compiler, kernel, process, operating system, schedule.

"Program", "line", "machine", "run", "wire", "circuit", "chapter", "lesson", "shop", "timer" and
"display" are free to use. The course's story is a shop with two freezer rooms, room A and room B,
each with a temperature sensor, and a display.

## The page around the items

From the top of the path down:

- The heading: "Signals to computer in five stages".
- Item 1 sits under the heading. Today it reads: "Module 0 comes first, before the five stages.
  The stages build the machine up from one wire, beginning in Module 1."
- Module 0's line: its name "What computers do", under it "Module 0", then its progress, then
  "Run a finished machine with a shop's program, line by line; open the machine level by level,
  down to one wire."
- The five stages' lines. Each shows its name, then the modules it holds, then its progress
  ("0 of 12 challenges complete"), then one line on what you do in it. Two of them read:
  - Programming, "Modules 10 and 11": "Learn why the machine's vocabulary is as it is; write
    programs for it; find their mistakes."
  - The whole machine, "Modules 12 and 13": "Make the machine respond when errors occur; run
    complete programs from start to end."
- New: the line for the optional chapters, after the five stages, in the same form: the name
  "Beyond the machine" (fixed, do not draft it), then item 2 where a stage names its modules,
  then its progress, then item 3. Pressed, it shows the chapters' lessons.

## Facts about the two chapters

- They are optional. The course is complete without them.
- They come after Module 13, the last module, and each needs the whole machine Modules 0 to 13
  built.
- Each chapter is one lesson. Neither has you build the thing it shows; each shows it at work on
  the course's own machine.
- The first: a program in the page reads a line written as the shop says it (for example, show on
  the display how much warmer room A is than room B) and writes, one at a time, the lines the
  machine runs for it. You then follow that run down into the machine's circuits.
- The second: two of the shop's programs share one machine. A timer takes the machine back from
  one program and gives it to the other, and each program later goes on from where it stopped,
  unaware.

## 1. The line under the heading

Item 1 today, quoted above. Redraft it so it also says that optional chapters come after the
five stages. At most two sentences. Keep "Module 0 comes first" and that the stages begin in
Module 1.

## 2. Where a stage names its modules

One or two words, saying the chapters are optional. Starts with a capital letter, no full stop.

## 3. What you do in the chapters

One line, in the form of the stages' lines quoted above: two short parts joined by a semicolon,
each starting with a verb, ending with a full stop. One part for each chapter, in the order above.
At most 120 characters.

## 4. The way in, line 1

The way in is a button at the top of the page, on two lines: line 1 says where it takes you, and
line 2 is the lesson's title, which is a question. For a module, line 1 reads "Continue with
Module 7" (for a reader who has done some challenges) or "Start with Module 7" (for a new reader).
Draft line 1 for a reader who has finished Module 13 and is offered the first optional chapter,
naming "Beyond the machine". Give both: the "Continue" form and the "Start" form. Each at most 40
characters, no full stop.

## 5. The links at the bottom of a lesson: the end

At the bottom of each lesson are two links: the lesson before, and the lesson after. After the
last lesson that exists today, the link after says "Next lesson is still to be written." above
"Back to the lessons". Draft the line that replaces "Next lesson is still to be written." on the
last optional chapter, the last lesson of the course: it says the course ends here. One short
sentence, ending with a full stop.
