# Brief 3A: lesson `fetch` (Module 8, lesson 3), first half

Read `00-module.md` (the shared fact sheet) first; it applies here, with one change: this lesson
introduces two terms, **program counter** and **fetch**, so PC, "program counter" and "fetch" are
allowed from here on. Introduce each in bold where the brief says. Use **instruction** and
**datapath** plainly.

Keys to write: `question`, `motivation`, `prediction`, `p1Question`, `p1Explain`, `marginLead`,
`marginAfter`.

## question (about 80 words)

1. In the last two lessons you put each instruction on IR by hand, and set WRITEY and BCONST
   yourself.
2. The shop's machine must do its jobs one after another on its own, one instruction per edge.
3. Module 6 built a ROM: words fixed when the circuit is made, read at an address. The machine
   keeps its list of instructions there: its program.
4. End on the question: at each edge, how does the machine know which instruction comes next?

## motivation (about 90 words)

1. The ROM holds 1024 bytes, at addresses `000` to `3FF`. An instruction is 4 bytes, stored low
   byte first at an address that is a multiple of 4. So the ROM holds 256 instructions.
2. A register holds the address of the next instruction. It is the **program counter**, PC.
   Introduce the term here, plain meaning first.
3. At each edge, PC takes PC + 4: the address of the instruction after this one.
4. A reset (RST at 1 at an edge) makes PC 0, so the first instruction is the one at `000`.

## prediction (above the figure; about 100 words)

1. The figure is the whole datapath so far, with the PC, the ROM, a "+ 4" block and two closed
   blocks the course supplies.
2. The "decoder" works out WRITEY, BCONST and the ALU's code from K and J, the signals you set by
   hand before. It is closed: Module 9 opens it.
3. The "stop logic" decides whether this edge stops the machine. It gives HALT (1 means the next
   edge stops the machine) and CAUSE, two hexadecimal digits that say why.
4. The ROM holds two instructions: `000` R1 ← 5 and `004` R1 ← R1 + 1, and no others. Every byte
   after them is 0. The figure has already made two edges: R1 is 6, PC is `008`, and IR carries
   `00000000`.
5. Choose an answer and press "Check my prediction". The "Clock edge" button appears after.

## p1Question (one or two sentences)

PC is `008`, and the ROM holds only 0s from there. What does the next edge do?

## p1Explain (about 90 words)

1. The machine stops, with cause 21.
2. The word at `008` is `00000000`. Its kind, 0, is no instruction: the decoder gives 21 on its
   output CAUSED, which says "illegal instruction".
3. The stop logic sets HALT to 1 and CAUSE to 21 before the edge.
4. With HALT at 1, the edge writes no register and PC keeps `008`. The simulator stops the clock
   and says why.
5. A machine that ran on would read 0s for ever. A program ends with the instruction `stop`
   instead.

## marginLead (above the investigation's figure; about 140 words, a list allowed)

1. The program works out the office's margin: how far room A's reading is above the limit -250.
   It is five instructions:
   - `000` `25001F06`: R1 ← -250
   - `004` `25002F48`: R2 ← -184
   - `008` `13213000`: R3 ← R2 - R1
   - `00C` `12334000`: R4 ← R3 + R3
   - `010` `84000000`: stop (kind 8, job 4)
2. The table "Program" lists each instruction with its address and marks the row PC names.
3. PC is a 64-bit register (block "register", named pc). Its output goes to the ROM's address and
   to the "+ 4" block, whose output PC4 comes back to PC's D.
4. Press "Clock edge" to make one edge, or "Run until it stops". The table "Buses" shows PC, PC4,
   IR and RESULT. Watch IR change as soon as PC does.

## marginAfter (below the figure; about 80 words)

1. The program stops after 5 edges, with PC at `010`: the `stop` instruction. Its cause is 00.
2. R1 holds -250, R2 -184, R3 66 and R4 132. Room A is 66 tenths of a degree above the limit.
3. At the stop, the decoder sets its output STOP to 1, and the stop logic sets HALT to 1. The edge
   after writes nothing, and PC stays `010`.
