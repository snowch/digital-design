# Brief R2-3: lesson `tracing` (13.3), after the reading review

Read `00-module.md` first; it applies. This lesson introduces **abstraction** (in `explanation`;
do not rewrite that key). You may use CPU, machine code, join and trace. Write only the keys
below. The ALU's output is drawn on the datapath as the wire RESULT; call it "the ALU's output,
RESULT", never "Y" (Y is the instruction's field for the register written). HR is drawn as the
block `heldR`.

## What changed on the page

- The figures no longer have "Pause before an edge" with two lists. Under the step buttons a strip
  shows the run, step by step: each step's line with its edges as buttons, numbered from the reset,
  by state ("28 ALU"). A press pauses the run before that edge. The step buttons and the line saying
  where the run is stay in reach at the top of the figure as the page scrolls.
- In the drawing, the wires the next edge changes are drawn thicker. A wire you press stays
  pinned: it is marked wherever it is drawn as you open blocks, and its name and value show under
  the drawing.
- The table of the levels opened lists each block opened with its maker and its ports' values.
- The question's figure is now paused before edge 54, the READ edge of `R2 <= R3`.
- The investigation's paragraph shows only once you have opened the half adder `ha2`.
- The construction's readings show only once the run has passed edge 48.
- The failure experiment's fault is now a carry held at 0: the carry from the ALU's group of four
  for bits 3 to 0 into its group for bits 7 to 4 (`C4` inside `g0`). Its own paragraph shows once
  its run has reached the end. Module 0's stuck wire was SUM in the slice for bit 1; this is not it.
- The challenge now asks four rows of bits, highest bit first, each from a trace.

## Keys

### `question` (about 110 words)

1. Module 0's second lesson followed one line of a program down to one wire, through nine levels,
   each named with the module that builds it, on a path chosen for you.
2. Lessons 1 and 2 showed every level of the whole machine at one edge.
3. This lesson's question: can you follow one value of your own choosing, at an edge of your own
   choosing, from a line of the program down to one gate?
4. The figure is paused before edge 54, the READ edge of `R2 <= R3`. Choose any other edge from the
   strip under the buttons.

### `motivation` (about 230 words; a short list allowed)

1. A **trace** follows one value down from a line of the program, through every level, to one gate
   (bold once, as a plain word).
2. The figure's tools, as a list:
   - the strip under the buttons pauses the run before any edge of any line;
   - a wire you press stays pinned: marked wherever it is drawn as you open blocks, its value under
     the drawing;
   - the table of the levels you have opened records each block, its maker and its ports' values at
     that edge;
   - "Parts here that never open" opens the parts the simulator runs whole.
3. Those parts: the register file, the memory, the PC, the IR, the held words, the control
   registers, the controller's state register, and the selectors and adders that take a whole word.
   Each shows one bit, drawn as the module that built that kind of part drew one bit: a register's
   bit as Module 5's; the register file's as one register's bit, Module 5's; a byte of the RAM's
   as a register's bit, Module 5's; a selector's as Module 3's; an adder's as Module 3's full
   adder. Its inputs carry the machine's own values at that edge.
4. A part that only splits a word into its bits or joins bits into a word has no gate; the list of
   parts that never open says so, and the trace passes through it.

### `sumAfter` (about 110 words; shown once you have opened `ha2`)

1. The levels from the top: the datapath (Module 8); the ALU (Module 7), whose output, RESULT, is
   `FFFFFFFFFFFFFFDE`, -34; inside the ALU its group of 16 bits, `g0`, and its group of 4, `q0`;
   the slice for bit 1; the full adder `fa` (Module 3); its half adder `ha2` (Module 3).
2. The 1 you followed is bit 1 of RESULT on the datapath, bit 1 of each group's result inside the
   ALU, and one wire at the bottom: the XOR gate `xorSum` gives SUM, 1.
3. The slice for bit 1 is called `bit1` inside its group of four; further up the word the names
   start again in each group: bit 6 of the word is `bit2` in group `q1`.

### `construction` (about 70 words; the route, no values)

The figure is paused before edge 48, the WRITE edge of `call R6, R15` at `02C`. Trace the PC's bit
3 through that call, as steps for the learner, each a question:
1. Show the PC's bit 3 from "Parts here that never open": what are D, EN and RST, and what does the
   flip-flop hold?
2. D comes from `nextTrap`, Module 12's choice of the next PC: open it; which input do its two
   selectors choose, and why?
3. That input comes from `next`, which Module 8 built: open it; what does `pickJump` choose?
4. Where does that value come from, and what is it?

### `pcAfter` (new; shown once the run has passed edge 48; a numbered list)

1. The PC's bit 3, as Module 5's register bit with a reset: D is 0, EN is 1, RST is 0, and the
   flip-flop holds 1. The PC holds `02C`, whose bit 3 is 1, and takes `034`, whose bit 3 is 0.
2. D comes from `nextTrap`: its two selectors both choose their first input, since TRAP is 0 and
   RESUME is 0.
3. That input comes from `next`: `pickJump` chooses RESULT, because JUMP is 1 for a call through a
   register.
4. RESULT is the ALU's output: R6 plus the constant 0, `034`.

### `failureExperiment` (about 70 words)

1. The figure holds one wire in the ALU at 0. Its label does not say which.
2. Under the drawing, the comparison with the model names the first instruction that disagrees.
3. Trace down from that wrong result to the wire that is held: read the result's bits against the
   model's, and open the ALU along the bits that differ.

### `faultAlu` (about 90 words; shown once the fault's run has reached its end; never state the
value of any wire inside the group for bits 3 to 0)

1. The comparison: after `R3 <= R1 - R2` at `010`, R3 is 50 on the machine and 66 by the model.
2. 66 is `0100 0010` in binary and 50 is `0011 0010`: bits 3 to 0 agree, bits 6 to 4 differ.
3. So the fault is where bits 7 to 4 are worked out, or what they take in. The trace goes from
   `datapath` to `alu` to `g0`. There the carry into the group for bits 7 to 4, `C4`, from the
   group for bits 3 to 0, is drawn held at 0.

### `generalisation` (about 80 words)

1. Every machine is built in levels, each a box over the one below. No one works on all the levels
   at once: a program uses instructions, an instruction uses blocks, a block uses gates.
2. The boxes make a machine possible to build and to understand; they also hide a fault until you
   open them.
3. A trace can start anywhere: a wrong value on the display, in a register, or on a lamp. Here it
   ends at a gate or a flip-flop, because the drawing of one bit opens no further. Module 4 opened
   the flip-flop itself into gates. (Do not announce the next lesson here.)

### `c1Task`

1. Each answer is a row of bits, the highest bit first, read at a named edge of a line of the shop's
   program. Use the figure under the challenge: pause at the edge, then trace. Set each bit with its
   button.
2. At the ALU edge of `R3 <= R1 - R2`, at `010`, in the ALU's group `q0` (the slices for bits 3 to
   0):
   - the output of the XOR gate `xorB` in each slice, bits 3 to 0 (it turns B's bit over for a
     subtraction);
   - the carry out of each slice's full adder, bits 3 to 0.
3. At the ALU edge of `goto R15`, at `040`: D of the PC's bits 5 to 2, as Module 5's drawing shows
   them. `goto R15` takes FETCH, READ and ALU, and the PC takes its next value at the ALU edge.
4. At the WRITE edge of `R5 <= R3 >= R4 signed`, at `018`: EN of bit 0 of each of R7 to R0, as the
   register file's bit shows it.
5. There are 4 tests, one for each row.

### `c1Hints.1` to `c1Hints.4` (the fifth is built from the answers; write `c1Whole` for it)

1. The idea: choose the edge from the strip under the buttons. Then open blocks until the wire is on
   the drawing; for a part that never opens, press its button and choose the bit.
2. A common mistake is to pause at the wrong edge of a line: a line's edges are in order from its
   FETCH edge, and for a register job the ALU edge is the third. Another is to write a row lowest
   bit first.
3. A smaller example, no part of the task: at the ALU edge of `R5 <= R3 >= R4 signed`, SUM in the
   slice for bit 1 gives 1, as the investigation found.
4. Part of the answer: the register file writes one register at an edge, and EN is 1 only for the
   register that Y names. At the ALU edge of `R3 <= R1 - R2`, B holds R2, -250, whose bits 3 to 0
   are `0110` before the XORs turn them over.
- `c1Whole`: the whole answer, the rows in the order of the answer boxes; `{answers}` stands for
  the four rows, comma-separated, and must appear once, as given.

### `reflection` (about 70 words)

1. You can now follow any value, at any edge, from a line of the program down to a gate or a
   flip-flop, and say what each level hides.
2. Every part you traced was the course's own, and so were the joins between them.
3. The next lesson asks: can you join the parts into the whole machine yourself, so that it runs
   the shop's programs as the model does?

### Feedback for a wrong row (`{actual}` once, as given; never a bit of the answer)

- `traceXorB`: pause at the ALU edge of `R3 <= R1 - R2`, open the ALU down to the group `q0`, and
  read `xorB` in each slice; it turns B's bit over when the ALU subtracts.
- `traceCarry`: at that same edge, open each slice's full adder and read its carry out, bit 3
  first.
- `tracePcD`: pause at the ALU edge of `goto R15`; show the PC's bits from the list of parts that
  never open; D is the bit of the address the jump goes to.
- `traceEn`: pause at the WRITE edge of `R5 <= R3 >= R4 signed`; show the register file's bit for
  each register; only one register is written at an edge.
