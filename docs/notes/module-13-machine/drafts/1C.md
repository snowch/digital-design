explanation: The whole machine is its parts and the joins between them. A join is a bus or a wire between two blocks. One block drives it, and another block reads it.

The control unit decides what each edge does. It decodes the instruction in the IR, steps the controller through its states, and sends every control signal on the control bus. The datapath holds the registers, the PC, the IR and the control registers, and does the arithmetic. The memory port holds the ROM, the RAM and the devices, behind one address.

Together, the control unit and the datapath work out and run each instruction. That part of the machine is the **CPU**, the central processing unit. The memory port, and everything behind it, sits outside the CPU.

A trap is the whole machine at work. The control unit sees the cause and sends TRAP. At the same edge, the datapath's control registers and its PC take their words, and the controller goes to FETCH.

The faults showed that a join can break the machine while every part still passes its own tests.

generalisation: Every machine is built this way: parts, each tested alone, then joined. A part's tests cannot test its joins.

The joins are tested by running whole programs. After every instruction, the machine is compared with the instruction-level model, which works out in numbers what each instruction should do, with no gates. The course has done this for every machine since Module 8.

The comparison names the first instruction that disagrees. That is where to start looking: at the joins and the parts that instruction uses.

The CPU and the memory are separate blocks joined by a few buses: an address out, a word out, a word back. The same split holds in machines far larger than this one.

c1Task: 1. Open the question's figure, or the failure experiment's, at its top level. For each of four buses, choose the block that drives it: the block the bus leaves from on its right side.
   - HB, the word a store writes;
   - WAITING, the events that wait for an interrupt;
   - STATUS, C0's two bits;
   - CAUSEM, the memory step's cause.
2. Use the investigation's timing diagram. Count edges from the reset, and find two edges:
   - the edge at which the IR takes `resume`'s word, `81000000`;
   - the edge at which the PC takes the return point, `040`.
3. There are 6 tests, one for each answer.

c1Hints.1: The idea: a bus is driven by the block whose right side it leaves. That block's outputs are on its right side. A bus enters the other blocks on their left side.

c1Hints.2: A common mistake: choosing the block a bus enters. A bus enters the block that reads it, not the one that drives it.

c1Hints.3: A smaller example: IR leaves the datapath on its right side and enters the control unit on its left. So the datapath drives IR.

c1Hints.4: Part of the answer: HB and STATUS leave the same block. `resume` takes three edges, FETCH, READ and WRITE. The IR takes its word at the first of them.

c1Hints.5: The whole answer: HB is driven by the datapath. WAITING is driven by the memory port. STATUS is driven by the datapath. CAUSEM is driven by the memory port. The IR takes `resume`'s word at edge 63, and the PC takes `040` at edge 65.

modelVsReality: A real CPU has the same blocks, but it runs several instructions at once. While one instruction is at its MEMORY edge, the next is at its ALU edge, and the one after is being fetched.

This machine runs one instruction at a time. So every edge belongs to one instruction.

Nothing of the overlapping is taught here. It is named so you know where this machine stops being like a real one.
