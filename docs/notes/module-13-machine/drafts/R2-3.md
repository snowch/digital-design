question: Module 0's second lesson followed one line of a program down to one wire. It passed through nine levels, each named with the module that builds it, on a path chosen for you. Lessons 1 and 2 showed every level of the whole machine at one edge.

This lesson asks a question of its own. Can you follow one value of your own choosing, at an edge of your own choosing, from a line of the program down to one gate?

The figure is paused before edge 54, the READ edge of `R2 <= R3`. Choose any other edge from the strip under the buttons.

motivation: Following one value from a line of the program, through every level, down to one gate is a **trace**. The figure gives you four tools for making one.

- The strip under the buttons pauses the run before any edge of any line.
- A wire you press stays pinned. It is marked wherever it is drawn as you open blocks, and its value shows under the drawing.
- The table of the levels you have opened records each block, its maker and its ports' values at that edge.
- The list "Parts here that never open" opens the parts the simulator runs whole.

Those parts are the register file, the memory, the PC, the IR, the held words, the control registers, the controller's state register, and the selectors and adders that take a whole word. Each shows one bit, drawn as the module that built that kind of part drew one bit. A register's bit is drawn as Module 5 drew it. The register file's bit is drawn as one register's bit, Module 5's. A bit of a byte of the RAM is drawn as a register's bit, Module 5's. A selector's bit is drawn as Module 3 drew it, and an adder's as Module 3's full adder. Each part's inputs carry the machine's own values at that edge.

A part that only splits a word into its bits or joins bits into a word has no gate. The list of parts that never open says so, and the trace passes through it.

sumAfter: Work down from the top. The datapath comes first, from Module 8. Inside it, the ALU from Module 7 gives RESULT, which is `FFFFFFFFFFFFFFDE`, or -34. Inside the ALU are a group of 16 bits, `g0`, and within that a group of 4, `q0`. The slice for bit 1 is `bit1` inside its group of four. It holds the full adder `fa` from Module 3, and that holds the half adder `ha2`, also from Module 3.

The 1 you followed is bit 1 of RESULT on the datapath, and bit 1 of each group's result inside the ALU. At the bottom, one wire is left. The XOR gate `xorSum` gives SUM, and SUM is 1.

The names start again in each group. Bit 6 of the word is `bit2` in group `q1`.

pcAfter: 1. The PC's bit 3, as Module 5's register bit with a reset: D is 0, EN is 1, RST is 0, and the flip-flop holds 1. The PC holds `02C`, whose bit 3 is 1, and takes `034`, whose bit 3 is 0.

failureExperiment: The figure holds one wire in the ALU at 0. Its label does not say which one. faultAlu: After `R3 <= R1 - R2` at `010`, R3 is 50 on the machine and 66 by the model. 66 is `0100 0010` in binary, and 50 is `0011 0010`. Bits 3 to 0 agree, and bits 6 to 4 differ.

So the fault is where bits 7 to 4 are worked out, or what they take in. The trace goes from `datapath` to `alu` to `g0`. There the carry into the group for bits 7 to 4, `C4`, from the group for bits 3 to 0, is drawn held at 0.

generalisation: Every machine is built in levels, each a box over the one below. No one works on all the levels at once. A program uses instructions, an instruction uses blocks, and a block uses gates.

The boxes make a machine possible to build and to understand. They also hide a fault until you open them.

A trace can start anywhere: a wrong value on the display, in a register, or on a lamp. Here it ends at a gate or a flip-flop, because the drawing of one bit opens no further. Module 4 opened the flip-flop itself into gates.
