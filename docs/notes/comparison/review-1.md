# Review 1: `remember` (A) and `registers` (B), read as a learner

Read from the lesson files and from the built site, served on port 4181 and walked with Playwright at 1280 and 375 pixels with every control pressed. Numbers were checked against the page, `library.ts` and both facts tests (18 of 18 pass). Severity follows the brief: high means a learner is misled or stuck, medium means a learner stumbles, low means polish. Where a fix would reveal a challenge's answer, the finding leaves the details out.

## Lesson A

**A1. Header, Objectives (fourth objective).** "Find the setup time of the flip-flop in the gate-delays model, see what the model says about the hold time, and say what the simulator cannot decide about a change just before the edge." This is the first thing a learner who knows only gates reads. One long sentence uses five things the lesson has not introduced yet (setup time, the flip-flop, the gate-delays model, hold time, the edge), most of them after a definite article. *Direction:* state the objective in words the learner already has, or split it. *Category:* term. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* medium (objectives may be meant as a preview).

**A2. Prediction, figure `predict-two` (`p1Question`).** "Here is a loop of two inverters with an OR gate on one input. The figure sets kick to 1 and then back to 0. What is q when kick returns to 0?" The figure draws no circuit: a prediction shows only its three options until "Check my prediction" is pressed. Nothing on the page has yet said which wire q is, and the section prose says only "an OR gate on the way round". While kick is 1, the wires round a two-inverter loop alternate between 0 and 1, so the right option depends on where q sits. The page shows where q sits only in the investigation that follows. The course's first prediction can be guessed but not reasoned out. *Direction:* let the learner see the loop, or say in words where q is, before asking. *Category:* figure. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A3. Prediction and Investigation (`p1Explain` and `investigationLoopTwo`; `p2Explain` and `investigationLoopThree`).** "the OR gate passes inverter 2's output unchanged to inverter 1, so the two inverters agree round the loop and nothing changes" is followed a few lines later by "When kick returns to 0, the OR gate passes inverter 2's output unchanged. The two inverters agree round the loop". In the same way, "Three inverters cannot agree with each other." is followed by "Three inverters cannot agree: the third contradicts the first." Each reveal and the investigation lead after it make the same argument back to back. "the third contradicts the first" is also vaguer than the reveal's "the value comes back inverted". *Direction:* let one text in each pair carry the argument, and give the other a job of its own, such as what the step slider shows. *Category:* repeat. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* high.

**A4. Investigation, figure `loop-three` (and figure `two-buttons`).** "Three inverters form a loop. When you kick and release, q is X." After kick and release, the status line says "These signals never settled and are shown as X: q, or.y, not1.y, not2.y.". The drawing, though, labels the loop 1, 0, 1, 0, and the "Inputs and outputs" table shows q as 0, at both widths. The two-button figure does the same after you press A, then B, then "Release all at once": the status says "These signals never settled and are shown as X: LIGHT, DARK.", while the drawing and table show LIGHT as 0. The stepped explorers draw the last settling step instead of the values the simulator marked X (`CircuitExplorer` shows `history[step]`). A learner checking the odd-loop claim sees a definite 0. *Direction:* fix the code first: the stepped view has to show the X that its status line promises. *Category:* figure. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A5. Investigation, figure `two-buttons` (the same drawing appears in `fault-lab` under "No fault").** Caption: "Press A and B to watch LIGHT and the gates in the two-button circuit." The first sentence of the lead says how each NOR gate is wired. In the drawing, B's wire climbs into the same horizontal track as A's wire, runs along it, and then passes under the norDark symbol. Between the pins and norDark the two buttons' wires are one line, so the drawing reads as if A and B were joined. In the SVG, both wires run along y = 36 from x = 58 to x = 100. The prose is right, but the figure beside it shows something else, and this is the lesson's central circuit. *Direction:* place the two-button drawing by hand, as the registers lesson's circuits are placed (`placed()` in `library.ts`), so that each button's wire can be seen running to its own gate. *Category:* figure. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A6. Investigation, `investigationTwoButtons`.** "The simulator settles in 2 steps." The figure says "Settled in 2 steps." after a press but "Settled in 0 steps." after a release. The page has also not yet said what a step is. That appears only in the "Time model" note at the foot of the page ("Every gate takes one step.") and in the note on how the simulator differs from hardware. The caption's "settling steps" has the same gap. *Direction:* tie the number to a press, and say what a step is where the learner first meets one. *Category:* term. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* high.

**A7. Construction, challenge "The two-button light".** "Build the light that remembers the last button, from NOR gates." **A8. Construction, `buildDLatchLead`.** "Its other output, the opposite of Q, is written Qb." The two-button circuit had one output, LIGHT. The other gate's output was an unnamed inner wire, so "its other output" points at something the learner never met as an output. The lead's second paragraph then repeats the task printed under it: "While EN is 1, Q copies D. While EN is 0, Q holds." sits above "When EN is 1, Q copies D. **A9. Construction and Challenge, hint ladders of "The follow-and-hold circuit" and "The flip-flop, drawn".** In both ladders, the second rung ("Common mistake") describes a wrong attempt so close to the right one that it gives away most of what the fourth rung ("Part of the answer") says. **A10. Failure experiment, `faultLabLead`.** "The checks fail at "release B" because the light comes back on instead of staying off." **A11. Failure experiment, figure `fault-lab`, with a fault chosen.** 'Choose "Feedback wire cut". The LIGHT gate reads X where the other gate's output was.' With this fault chosen, the drawing is laid out afresh. The two gates swap places (norLight moves to the left), nothing marks the cut, and norLight's second input has no wire at all. **A12. Failure experiment, `dLatchExplorerLead`, and the section's place in the arc.** "A signal that rises and falls at a steady rate, used to time a whole circuit, is called a clock, written CLK." This arrives as a definition beside a D latch figure that has no clock. "If Q feeds the D of a second latch and both latches share one EN, a change races through both while EN is 1." describes two latches, but the figure has one, so the race is told, not shown. The next sentence then jumps from a shared EN to "once per rise of the clock" without saying that the EN is the clock. "The fix is two D latches in series with an inverter. This is the course's flip-flop." names the flip-flop before saying what it does. The next figure then measures its setup and hold, before the explanation section opens it up. The latch answers the lesson's question (can gates remember?). The second half, the flip-flop and its timing, has no question of its own and sits inside the failure experiment. *Direction:* give the flip-flop a question and a section (or a lesson) of its own, and let the learner see the race before the fix. *Category:* arc. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**A13. Failure experiment, `setupHoldLead`.** "Setup time in this model is 30 units. Hold time here is zero: a change at the edge or after is simply missed." This lesson introduces setup and hold as rationed terms, but neither is ever put in plain words: how long before the edge D must be steady, and how long after it D must stay. The learner has to infer each definition from the numbers. "simply" is an intensifier. *Direction:* give the plain meaning first, then the number. *Category:* term. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**A14. Failure experiment, `setupHoldLead`.** "The roll declares Q undecided 20 units after the edge, which is when the slave's latch would first have answered." The slave is introduced in the explanation section, three figures later, and "the slave's latch" also speaks of a latch's latch. In the same paragraph, "a recorded seed" comes two sentences before "A seed is a number...". "This is called metastable." uses an adjective as the name of a state. *Direction:* move the slave detail after the internals figure or say it without the term, and define "seed" at its first use. *Category:* term. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**A15. Failure experiment, figure `setup-hold`.** Prose: "If D settled 30 or more units before the edge, Q changes cleanly 30 units after the edge." Figure: "Q captured 1 at 1030.", and after a roll, "Roll 1: Q became undecided at 1020, settled to 0 at 1060." The prose counts units from the edge, but the figure gives absolute times, and nothing (no axis, no label) says the edge is at 1000. The quoted sentence also uses 30 for two different things, before and after the edge, and three sentences later the paragraph adds "Setup time in this model is 30 units." *Direction:* use one frame of reference for prose and figure, and make the edge's time visible. *Category:* fact. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**A16. Failure experiment, figure `setup-hold`, the roll.** "A seed is a number that fixes the whole sequence, so a replay of the same roll gives the same picture and a new roll gets a new seed." With D at 20 units before the edge, pressing "Roll result" three times gives "Roll 1", then "Roll 2", then "Roll 2" again with the same outcome (settled to 0 at 1066). Every later press repeats it. A roll at 25 units also comes out as "Roll 2", after which the "Rolls" list shows two identical "Roll 2" lines. The cause is in `SetupHold.tsx`: the seed is the number of stored rolls plus one, and a new roll at the same position replaces the old one. A learner who keeps rolling to see that "nothing predicts which or how long" gets the same picture every time. *Direction:* fix the code first. *Category:* fact. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A17. Failure experiment, figure `setup-hold`, labels.** "The roll button rolls an outcome from a recorded seed." The button, "Roll result", exists only while the slider is inside the band. At the default position (80 units before the edge) it is absent, and the prose does not say so. The band also has three names: "The band" in the prose, "Uncertain" on the figure, and "the window" in the status line ("D changed inside the window"). *Direction:* say where the button appears, and give the band one name. *Category:* label. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* high.

**A18. Explanation, figure `internals` (the drawing).** The lead says which clock signal reaches each latch's EN, and the caption asks the learner to "open the master and the slave". In the drawing, the wire into the slave's EN runs through the inverter and straight through the master's box, leaving it at the master's Qb port, so the slave's EN appears to be driven by the master's Qb. The two wires into the master's EN share their last segment, and D's wire runs through the inverter symbol. All of this was measured from the SVG. Which clock goes where is left out, because that is the flip-flop challenge's answer. This is the figure that is meant to show the mechanism. *Direction:* place the flip-flop's inner drawing by hand. *Category:* figure. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A19. Explanation, figure `internals`, phase captions (`phases`).** "D fell at time 550 while CLK was 1." The figure's own values table shows CLK as 0 at time 550. CLK fell at 500, as the same caption says two sentences later, after it has already described 550. The caption's next sentence, about the master at 550, does not match the master Q trace either (the trace's times are pinned in `lesson-facts.test.ts`). The recorded run never changes D while CLK is 1, so the lead's sentence about what happens when D changes while CLK is 1 is never shown, yet this caption claims it is. The captions also lag the clock. "CLK rose at time 100. The slave opened..." stays on screen while CLK is 0, from 200 to 299, and the caption for 400 stays on screen past the fall at 500. *Direction:* correct the caption against the trace, and either add a change of D while CLK is 1 to the script or drop the claim. *Category:* fact. *Severity:* high. *Sure:* high.

**A20. Explanation, `internalsAfter`.** "The numbers in the previous section are what those gate delays add up to in this model: 30 and 10, where 30 is three gate delays." The previous section gives a setup time of 30 and a hold time of zero, as well as Q changing 30 units after the edge, the band from 25 to 15, and misses from 10 units before the edge. **A21. Explanation, tables `table-sr` and `table-d` (and the D latch table in the failure experiment).** "S=1 and R=0 set Q to 1. S=0 and R=1 reset Q to 0. S=0 and R=0 hold the value. S=1 and R=1 force both outputs to 0." is read out above a table that shows the same four rows. One case has three names, and here "input" means a combination of inputs. *Direction:* introduce one name, once, and let each lead say what its table does not. *Category:* repeat. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* high.

**A22. Generalisation, `asTextLead`, and table `table-dff`.** "`always_ff` means at every clock edge." It is `@(posedge CLK)` that chooses the edge, as the next sentence explains. Read in order, the learner is told first that the block runs at every edge, perhaps both kinds, and only then that it is the rising one. The flip-flop table further down is titled "D flip-flop (edge-triggered)", and its rows say "Captures 0". The prose uses neither word; it says "takes D". *Direction:* let the keyword and the edge it waits for each mean one thing, and match the table's words to the prose. *Category:* fact. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* medium.

**A23. Generalisation, `asTextAfter`, and Reflection.** "Many flip-flops sharing one clock hold several bits at once." This answers the reflection's "What would it take to hold eight bits instead of one?" two sections before it is asked. "A circuit whose next value depends on its present value and its inputs is the next lesson's subject." The next lesson on the site (`content/lessons/index.ts` orders remember, then registers) asks how a circuit keeps several bits together, and itself ends by pointing at logic that computes the next value. "Real flip-flops have a hold time as well, and the course's later lessons say more." **A24. Whole page, "hold".** "While EN is 0, Q holds." uses it to mean keeping a value. "Hold EN at 1 and change D.", "off while A is held", "Button A held down" and "While CLK is held low or high, Q stays the same." use it to mean keeping an input at one level. **A25. Whole page, "X".** The option says "The simulator cannot decide (X)". Later the page says "Before any kick, q is X: the simulator has nothing to go on." **A26. How the simulator differs from hardware.** "The simulator decides every race by the order it visits gates. Hardware decides by physics." The fault lab says "The simulator cannot decide which gate wins, so LIGHT is X.", and the figure shows X. **A27. Whole page, small style points.** "This is called metastable." names a state with an adjective, and the hardware note then says "metastability". **A28. Figures `loop-two` and `loop-three`, and the challenge "The D latch, as text".** Both inverter-loop explorers carry a "Release all at once" button, although each has only one input; the fault lab is the only figure that uses it. **A29. Section headings.** "How a circuit remembers" heads the section that asks the question, not one that answers it. "Faults, transparency problems, edge timing" and "Feedback loops, tables, two-stage latches" are lists. "two-stage latches" is a name for the flip-flop that the lesson uses nowhere else. *Direction:* follow style.md rule 7: a heading says what its section contains. *Category:* label. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* medium.

**A30. Originality.** The circuits and their order are the standard ones: two cross-coupled inverters that hold a value, an odd loop that never settles, the NOR SR latch, the D latch made by gating S and R, the master-slave flip-flop built from two D latches, then setup, hold and metastability. The two-button task, the kick and the recorded roll are the lesson's own; the skeleton is not. *Direction:* decide whether the originality note's claim holds for the sequence as well as for the framing. *Category:* originality. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* medium.

## Lesson B

**B1. Header, Objectives (first objective).** "Build a register from flip-flops sharing one clock, and say why all bits change at the same moment." No task asks the learner to build one: the four-flip-flop register is shown in the investigation and written as text in the last challenge. *Direction:* match the verb to what the learner actually does. *Category:* label. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* high.

**B2. **B4. Prediction (section prose) and elsewhere.** "The timing diagram that appears shows what the simulator did." **B5. Prediction (`p2Explain`) and How the simulator differs from hardware.** "The lesson comes back to this with a reset." **B6. Investigation, `fourFlipFlopsLead`.** "Below are four flip-flops from the previous lesson, arranged ff3 to ff0 from top to bottom, the order a word is written." The term "word" is defined only at the end of the same lead ("Several bits kept together and treated as one value are a word."). At this point a learner reads it as an ordinary word. **B7. Whole page, "keep".** "one flip-flop keeps one bit", "Four flip-flops keep four bits" and "Flip-flops that share one clock and keep a word are a register." use it to mean storing at all. "At a rising edge where EN is 0, Q keeps the value it had", the keep path and the KEEP wire use it to mean taking Q instead of D at an edge. "Keep one clock for everything." and "This course keeps one rule from here" use it to mean sticking to something. The first two meanings collide on one screen. The register is defined as flip-flops that "keep a word", and the very next paragraph says "This register still takes D at every edge" and "You need a way to tell each edge whether to take D or keep the word." The title's "keep several bits together" is yet another sense. *Direction:* give the meaning "retain at an edge" a word of its own, or stop using "keep" for storing. *Category:* double meaning. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**B17. Explanation (section prose), "D".** "At every rising edge, every flip-flop takes its D. EN decides what D becomes. When EN is 1, D is the new value." In the bit the learner has just built, D is also the circuit's input, while the flip-flop's D pin is driven by the gates in front of it. Read with the input in mind, "EN decides what D becomes" says that EN changes the input. The first hint of the first challenge uses D in the same double way. *Direction:* name the pin and the input differently wherever both are meant. *Category:* double meaning. *Severity:* medium. *Sure:* high.

**B18. Motivation, Prediction, Explanation, `keepClearBitLead`, and How the simulator differs from hardware.** The case for a reset is made five times. **B22. **B26. Originality.** The sequence is the standard one. Four flip-flops on one clock make a register. An enable goes in front of each D, with a warning against gating the clock. A synchronous reset is one more AND gate in front of D, taking priority over the enable. Then comes a shift register that takes bits in one at a time. As I recall, Harris and Harris treat enabled and resettable flip-flops in this order, warning against clock gating included, but I could not check the book from here. The Save-button display, the prediction that an enable alone leaves Q unknown, and the edge the clock never made are the lesson's own. *Direction:* the same as A30. *Category:* originality. *Severity:* low. *Sure:* medium.

## Comparison

Lesson B reads better for its learner. Three things decide it.

First, B's predictions name every signal before they ask. Lesson A's first prediction asks for q without drawing the loop or saying which wire q is.

Second, B's figures show what its prose claims, apart from the wire-press instruction and the flip-flop drawing it shares with A. In A, six things contradict the text beside them: the odd loop showing q as 0, the button wires, the flip-flop's drawing, its phase captions, the repeating roll and the hardware note on races.

Third, B follows one question through predict, build, break, explain and generalise. A answers its question with the latch, then adds the clock, the flip-flop, setup and hold inside the failure experiment.

B's faults are mostly words: "keep" and "D" each mean two things.
