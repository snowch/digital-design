## question (about 100 words)

1. Lesson 1 asked why every field keeps one place in every instruction, and what that costs.
2. Every instruction is 32 bits, written as eight hexadecimal digits: the kind K, the job J, the
   registers A, B and Y, and the constant in digits 2 to 0. Each field sits in the same digits in
   every instruction.
3. Why whole digits: there are sixteen registers, sixteen kinds and sixteen jobs, so each field
   needs exactly 4 bits, one hexadecimal digit.
4. Cutting the word by bits instead would buy room only where a field is unused.
5. End on the questions: what does keeping every field in one place give? What does it cost?

## layoutsAfter (a list; about 90 words)

In the packed layout:
1. A register job: no change. It uses every register digit and leaves the constant unused.
2. A branch: a 16-bit constant (-32768 to 32767), and nothing moves. The word stays `56230002`.
   A store is laid out as a branch is: A and B, then four digits of constant.
3. A jump: a 20-bit constant (-524288 to 524287), and nothing moves.
4. A constant job and a load: a 16-bit constant, and Y moves to digit 4, as the prediction found.
5. A call: a 20-bit constant, and Y moves to digit 5.

## construction (about 80 words)

1. To write an instruction's word, write its fields in order, one digit each: K, J, A, B, Y, then
   the constant as three hexadecimal digits.
2. A field the instruction does not use is written as 0.
3. A negative constant is written as the 12 bits whose signed reading it is: -100 is `F9C`.
4. Example: R2 ← R1 + 100 is kind 2 (constant job), job 2 (add), A is R1, B unused (0), Y is R2,
   and 100 is `064`. The word is `22102064`.

## c1Hint2 (one hint)

A common mistake: the constant in decimal, or with a minus sign. In hexadecimal, 16 is `010`, and
-100 is `F9C`.

## packedReadAfter (about 80 words)

1. Each word writes R0, not the register it was written for.
2. The course's machine reads digit 3 as Y. In `22120064` and `380207D8`, digit 3 is the constant's
   top digit. In `60F00001`, the constant is digits 4 to 0, and digit 3 is its second digit. In all
   three it is 0.
3. `22120064` reads as R0 ← R1 + 100; `380207D8` as R0 ← memory[`7D8`]; `60F00001` as R0 ← PC + 4
   and PC ← PC + 4 × 1.
4. A word means one thing only under one layout. The layout is part of the instruction set.

## explanation (about 130 words)

1. K and J together say what an instruction does: its kind and its job. The bits that choose what
   an instruction does are what books call its **opcode**. In the course's layout, the opcode is
   digits 7 and 6.
2. Both layouts keep the opcode in digits 7 and 6, in every instruction. So K is always in the
   same place, and it can choose which digit is Y: that is the packed layout's selector, the one
   the challenge writes.
3. The packed layout gives longer constants: 16 bits for a constant job, a load, a store or a
   branch; 20 bits for a call or a jump.
4. It pays with selectors: Y sits in digit 3, 4 or 5 by kind, so the register file's write address
   needs a selector chosen by K. The constant is 12, 16 or 20 bits, so the widening needs one too.
5. On the course's machine the longer branch buys nothing: a 12-bit branch already reaches the
   whole ROM (lesson 3 says why). Only a constant job's constant would gain.

## modelVsReality (about 60 words)
