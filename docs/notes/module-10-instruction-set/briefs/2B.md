# Brief 2B: lesson `encoding` (Module 10, lesson 2), second part

Read `00-module.md` (the shared fact sheet) first; it applies here. "Instruction set" and
"microarchitecture" are allowed. This lesson introduces **opcode** in the explanation, which
another brief writes: "opcode" must not appear in these keys. "Immediate" must not appear.

Keys to write: `investigation`, `layoutsLead`, `layoutsAfter`, `explorerLead`, `explorerAfter`,
`construction`, `writeWordsLead`, `c1Task`, `c1Hints.1` to `c1Hints.5`, `packedReadLead`,
`packedReadAfter`.

## investigation (one or two sentences, above two figures)

The first figure compares the two layouts for six instructions. The second takes any word apart,
and runs Module 7's ALU.

## layoutsLead (above the first figure; about 50 words)

1. Choose an instruction. The figure shows its word in each layout, digit by digit, with each
   field's name over its digits.
2. Under each layout it gives the constant's width and range. A field that moves is outlined.

## layoutsAfter (below the first figure; about 110 words, a list allowed)

1. In the packed layout:
   - a constant job and a load: a 16-bit constant, -32768 to 32767, and Y moves to digit 4;
   - a branch: a 16-bit constant and nothing moves; the word stays `56230002`;
   - a call: a 20-bit constant, -524288 to 524287, and Y moves to digit 5;
   - a jump: a 20-bit constant, and nothing moves;
   - a register job: no change. It uses every register digit, and leaves the constant unused.
2. A store is laid out as a branch is: A and B, then four digits of constant.

## explorerLead (above the second figure; about 130 words)

1. Type a word of eight hexadecimal digits, or choose one below the box.
2. The figure shows the word's fields, what the machine makes of it, and its constant widened to
   64 bits as the machine widens it.
3. Below is the calculator. It runs Module 7's ALU in the simulator, at 16 or 64 bits.
4. Type A and B as numbers read signed or as hexadecimal, and choose a job. The calculator gives Y
   in bits, in hexadecimal, unsigned and signed, and the four flags: ZERO, MINUS, COUT and OVER.
5. "Take the job and B from the word" sets the job from the J of a register job or a constant job.
   For a constant job it also sets B to the widened constant.

## explorerAfter (below the second figure; about 60 words)

1. The calculator starts with the rooms' readings, A -184 and B -250, and job 3, subtract.
2. Y is 66. ZERO is 0, MINUS 0, COUT 1 and OVER 0.
3. These are the flags the colder-room program's branch reads. MINUS XOR OVER is 0, so -184 is not
   less than -250, read signed.

## construction (about 90 words)

1. To write an instruction's word, write its fields in order, one digit each: K, J, A, B, Y. Then
   write the constant as three digits.
2. A field the instruction does not use is written 0.
3. A negative constant is written as its 12-bit signed reading: -3 is `FFD`.
4. An example: R2 ← R1 + 100. Kind 2 (a constant job), job 2 (add), A is R1, B unused (0), Y is
   R2, and 100 is `064` in hexadecimal. The word is `22102064`.

## writeWordsLead (one sentence)

Write each instruction as its eight hexadecimal digits.

## c1Task (about 100 words, a list allowed)

1. Write each instruction's word as eight hexadecimal digits:
   - R5 ← R2 - 7, a constant job with the ALU's subtract;
   - R4 ← memory[R1 + 16], a load of a word;
   - if R3 != R6, PC ← PC + 4 × (-3): a branch three instructions back.
2. Write 0 in a field the instruction does not use.
3. There are 3 tests, one for each word. A failed test shows the fields your word holds, beside
   the instruction asked for.

## c1Hints (five, in this order)

1. The idea: one digit for each of K, J, A, B and Y, then the constant's three digits.
2. A common mistake: writing the constant in decimal, or with a minus sign. 16 is `010`, and -3 is
   `FFD`.
3. A smaller example: R2 ← R1 + 100 is `22102064`.
4. Part of the answer: a load of a word at RA + c is kind 3, job 0. A branch taken when A differs
   from B is kind 5, job 3. Subtract is job 3.
5. The whole answer: `23205007`, `30104010` and `53360FFD`.

## packedReadLead (above the failure figure; about 90 words)

1. The course's machine reads every word in the course's layout. What does it make of a word
   written in the packed layout?
2. The figure offers three packed words:
   - `22120064`, R2 ← R1 + 100;
   - `380207D8`, R2 ← memory[`7D8`];
   - `60F00001`, R15 ← PC + 4 and PC ← PC + 4 × 1, a call.
3. Choose each one. Before you do, say which register the course's machine will write.

## packedReadAfter (below the figure; about 80 words)

1. Each word writes R0, not the register it was written for.
2. The course's machine reads digit 3 as Y. In each packed word, digit 3 holds the constant's top
   digit, here 0.
3. `22120064` reads as R0 ← R1 + 100. `380207D8` reads as R0 ← memory[`7D8`]. `60F00001` reads as
   R0 ← PC + 4 and PC ← PC + 4 × 1.
4. A word means one thing only under one layout. The layout is part of the instruction set.
