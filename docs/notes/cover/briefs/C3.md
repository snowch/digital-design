# Brief C3: the cover's proposition, its path in what you build, and what a page without scripts says

You are drafting words for the cover at the top of the front page of an interactive course,
*Digital Design: From Bits to a Working Computer*, and for the short text a search result or a
shared link shows. A reader meets the cover before every lesson, including before the first, so it
must not use any word that a lesson introduces as a term (list below). Read `docs/style.md` in the
repository before you write: it is the style checklist, and it applies to every sentence. Use only
the facts here. Where a sentence seems to need a fact you were not given, write a note in square
brackets instead of inventing it. Do not write or change any file.

## Why these words are being redrafted

A first-time visitor's review of the site said the page must tell a newcomer, within 30 seconds,
that they are going to build a computer, not merely read about one, and that the course's way of
teaching is what sets it apart. The present opening paragraph has the facts but buries them: its
first sentence runs past 40 words. The five stages of the path describe topics; a newcomer should
read in each one what they will build.

## Voice

British English. Direct, plain, active, short. The reader is "you". No marketing tone, no slogans,
no exclamation marks, no rhetorical questions, no filler, no em dashes or en dashes used as dashes.
"Press" for buttons, never "click" or "tap". Numbers as digits.

## Words the cover must not use

These are terms a lesson introduces; the cover comes before them. Do not use them, nor their
plurals or other forms: bit, binary, word, hexadecimal, signed, unsigned, gate, truth table,
Boolean expression, XOR, XNOR, NAND, universal, threshold, noise margin, one-hot, carry, half
adder, full adder, overflow, multiplexer, bus, decoder, demultiplexer, encoder, comparator, depth,
feedback, latch, transparent, edge, synchronous, register, shift register, register transfer,
register file, counter, state machine, state diagram, next-state logic, address, RAM, ROM, byte,
aligned, alignment, memory-mapped, ALU, flag, instruction, instruction set, encoding, immediate,
opcode, program counter, fetch, datapath, branch, control unit, illegal instruction, instruction
register, micro-operation, SystemVerilog. "AND", "OR" and "NOT" may be used, as the module names
do. `assumes` (below) is the one exception: it must name binary, since that is what the course
assumes.

## 1. The opening paragraph (`lead`)

It sits under the course's title, beside a live circuit the reader can press, and above the button
that starts the course. The facts, each true of the course today:

- You build a working computer from its parts.
- You start from two voltages on a wire.
- Each part you build is used again in the next thing you build. By Module 8 the parts you have
  built join into one machine that runs programs.
- For each circuit, you predict what it will do, build it, run it, break it on purpose, and explain
  what happened.
- Each new idea, and each new name, arrives when the circuit you are building raises the question
  that needs it, not before.
- Every figure that simulates runs the real circuit. None is an animation.
- Everything runs in your browser, and your work stays in your browser.

Return `lead`: at most 60 words, in 3 to 5 short sentences. The first sentence says what you build,
in at most 12 words. Keep every fact.

## 2. The five stages of the path (`stage1About` to `stage5About`)

Under the opening, the course's path runs in five stages, each with a short name (kept as they
are: Signals, Memory, Machine, Programming, The whole machine), a line under it, and the modules
it covers (the page adds "Modules 1 to 3" and so on itself). Each line should say what you build
or do in that stage, as an instruction to the reader that starts with a verb. The facts for each:

1. Signals, Modules 1 to 3 ("Voltage to numbers", "Learning AND, OR, NOT", "Selecting, comparing,
   adding"): you turn two voltages on a wire into numbers; you build circuits that combine signals
   with AND, OR and NOT; then circuits that choose one signal of several, compare two numbers, and
   add them.
2. Memory, Modules 4 to 6 ("Memory and time", "Counting and sequences", "Accessing many numbers"):
   you build circuits that keep a value until told to change it; circuits that count and step
   through a sequence; a memory that keeps many numbers, each found by its own number.
3. Machine, Modules 7 to 9 ("Arithmetic and logic", "Putting pieces together", "Control and
   sequencing"): you build the circuit that does arithmetic and logic on whole numbers; you join
   your parts into one machine that runs a program; you build the control that takes the machine
   through each step.
4. Programming, Modules 10 and 11 ("The machine's vocabulary", "Programming and debugging"): you
   learn the words the machine understands, and why they are as they are; you write programs for
   your machine and find their mistakes.
5. The whole machine, Modules 12 and 13 ("Errors and responses", "The whole machine"): you make the
   machine respond when a program goes wrong; you run programs from start to end on the finished
   machine.

Return `stage1About` to `stage5About`: each one line of at most 14 words, starting with a verb,
with no full stop needed.

## 3. What the course assumes (`assumes`)

It sits under the button. Today it reads: "The course assumes you can turn a binary number into
decimal and a decimal number into binary." The facts: the course assumes you can turn a binary
number into decimal and a decimal number into binary; it assumes everyday arithmetic; it assumes
nothing about electronics, circuits or programming.

Return `assumes`: one or two short sentences.

## 4. The text a search result or a shared link shows (`description`)

Search engines and the previews of shared links show the course's title, then this text under it.
It is the only text many people see before they decide whether to open the page. The facts: you
build a working computer from its parts, starting from two voltages on a wire; for each circuit
you predict, build, run, break and explain; it all runs in your browser.

Return `description`: at most 155 characters, in one or two sentences. Do not repeat the title.

## 5. A page's name in the browser's tab (`pageTitle`)

Every page now shows "Digital Design" in the browser's tab, so a reader with several lessons open
cannot tell them apart. Each page's tab will show the page's own name (a lesson's title, which is
its question, or "Before you start"), then the course's short name, "Digital Design". The front
page keeps the full title.

Return `pageTitle`: the pattern, written with `{page}` where the page's own name goes, using a
separator that is neither an em dash nor an en dash.

Return each key in the form `key: text`, one per line, with a blank line between keys, and nothing
else.
